## Page 1: The Making of GRAVITY FORCE

*Source: https://www.amigafuture.de/app.php/kb/viewarticle?a=3226*

### Gravity Fever

**10 years ago, an Amiga game appeared that looked insignificant at first glance – but it was to quickly develop into a cult game.**

**We are talking about Gravity Force – two-player chase through narrow mazes, with gliders that only had an anti-gravity drive... and a nice laser cannon.**

**Even today, people still like to bring it out every now and then, because who knows: perhaps an even better time can be achieved with an even more breakneck flying style?**

**...We tracked down the programmer of this game, and he reports for us how the game was created.**

---

*by Stephan Wenzler*

The year was 1988. PCs were still carrying out their monotonous duties in gray offices, "GDR" was an insignificant acronym, and I was a 17-year-old amateur with a big vision: I wanted to program a game for the Amiga that would make people happy, make me rich, and that humanity would still talk about in 10 years. I had owned an Amiga 500 since October '87 and it was time to program it.

At the beginning, that was a pretty confusing matter if you had only tinkered around a little on the C64 before. For a moment I even seriously considered dropping the whole thing (unthinkable!), and doing something more sensible and simpler instead. Eventually, however, all the confusion cleared up noticeably (bless *Amiga Intern*), and armed with the Seka Assembler, I felt ready to tackle my great task.

### A good game... but what kind?

I was still missing something important: a usable game concept. I started thinking, and it was quickly clear that I wanted to make something like *Thrust*. Inspiration from other games simply cannot always be avoided. *Thrust* was, by the way, inspired by *Gravitar*, which I didn't know at the time, though.

The ball hanging from the ship in *Thrust* seemed too complicated to me from the start; besides, I wanted a different gameplay principle anyway: you were supposed to rescue little men (a la *Choplifter*). Eventually, however, the realization took hold that collecting crates would actually do the trick as well. Besides, that was easier to program—after all, crates don't run around. Incidentally, back then only those green tanks were planned as enemies. So the original concept was set. Shortly afterwards, I was already able to do the first laps with my rocket ship; compared to *Thrust*, I had designed the ship dynamics to be a bit more agile, and I saw that it was good.

## Page 2: Kult-Corner – Creation of the Cult Game

*Screenshot caption: Insane races in 2-player mode!*[cite: 2]

For level construction, I was inspired by the character mode of the C64: Each level was composed of 8x8 pixel, beautifully drawn blocks for background graphics, and (in-game) invisible blocks for control purposes[cite: 2]. Some of the control characters caused, for example, a specific cannon to start shooting when the ship was above it[cite: 2]. That was an elegant method, I thought[cite: 2]. In total, there were 256 characters, which gave the graphics a certain simplicity, but that didn't bother me yet[cite: 2].

I continued to focus on good gameplay[cite: 2]. Bit by bit, more elements were added, such as the aforementioned shooting cannons[cite: 2]. Nevertheless, collecting crates in a brown cave was slowly but surely becoming too boring for me, and I pondered what else I could do with my rocket ship[cite: 2]. Finally, I came up with the idea of racing through numbered gates[cite: 2].

It must have been around this time that a far more dramatic upheaval began to draw its misshapen outlines on the horizon[cite: 2]. An idea that would influence generations of game programmers for years to come, though I didn't suspect it at the time[cite: 2]. The race against the clock wasn't bad, but what if you could compete live against a second player, I wondered[cite: 2]. A two-player mode was needed—with brotherly split screen[cite: 2]. Implementing the split screen wasn't even that complex, which I naturally welcomed[cite: 2].

The result was stunning: Now you could fly races against each other and shoot each other down—shoot each other down?[cite: 2] That was to become the cue for the next expansion[cite: 2]. I could no longer tolerate these orgies of senseless violence in 2-player races: I had something higher in mind: I wanted to create an orderly framework for a man-to-man fight to the last ship[cite: 2]. The "Dogfight" mode was born[cite: 2]. This, I thought, would be a nice little bonus[cite: 2].

### Where will the game appear?

My little game was now 90% finished[cite: 2]. It was slowly time to check its commercial viability[cite: 2]. Kingsoft had an advertisement back then looking for new software, so a demo went to Kingsoft, and just to be safe, simultaneously to two other publishers[cite: 2]. To my great surprise, two of them accepted spontaneously, including Kingsoft[cite: 2]. After tough negotiations, Kingsoft won the deal[cite: 2].

The name "Gravity Force" was Kingsoft's idea, by the way; otherwise, however, they had no further influence on the game, and I was able to complete the remaining 10% undisturbed[cite: 2]. In early 1989, Gravity Force hit the market... the rest is history[cite: 2].

### Who could have guessed?

If anyone could make an accusation against me, it was that I underestimated the importance of the 2-player mode and focused too much on the traditional part[cite: 2]. However, big innovations simply take time, and I also wanted to get finished at some point[cite: 2].

By the way, I saw the many imitations as the best compliments I could get, even if I didn't get really rich[cite: 2]. Maybe there will soon be an official (really good) sequel from me[cite: 2].
